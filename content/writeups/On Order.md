Order emerges from the self-organization of dissipative structures, a phenomenon that is ubiquitous: Rayleigh-Bénard convection[^11] in fluid thermodynamics (regular cloud formations in the sky[^5], solar granules[^13], the hexagonal basalt columns of the Giant's Causeway[^12], mantle convection[^14]), the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction[^10] in chemical systems, and everything we know well — climate, cities, flames, explosions, stock markets, traffic, bee swarms, ecosystems, metabolism, life, consciousness.

![stone](https://jipeng4974.github.io/img/stone.webp)

A dissipative structure is a steady-state system far from equilibrium that continuously exchanges matter and energy with its environment. Dissipative structures arise from input and dissipation: when energy or matter flows into a system, if there is a suitable mechanism to dissipate the excess energy, it can lead to a local increase in order or the formation of regularity. As dissipation continues, a complexity — or "intricacy" — that did not exist at first is born in the dissipative system: new behaviors, structures, and patterns arise on their own, organizing spontaneously, assembling automatically, running autonomously.

At equilibrium, the measurable macroscopic physical properties of a system are uniform everywhere, and no macroscopic irreversible processes occur inside the system; it obeys the first law of thermodynamics (the increase in internal energy = heat absorbed - work done on the surroundings), the second law of thermodynamics (spontaneous entropy increase of the system), and Boltzmann's ordering principle (in a system at temperature T, the fraction of subsystems with internal energy E is e-E/kT). Near-equilibrium systems lie in the linear regime not far from equilibrium, obeying the Onsager reciprocal relations (a set of formulas describing the relationships among the various fluxes and forces in the system's transport processes) and the principle of minimum entropy production (given boundary conditions constraining a system as it approaches equilibrium, the system settles into the state of least dissipation, i.e., minimum entropy production).

"Far from equilibrium" is a state, relative to equilibrium and near-equilibrium, in which the measurable physical properties of the system are extremely non-uniform; in this state the system's thermodynamic behavior defies the principle of minimum entropy production and heads toward high entropy production and macroscopic order.

Compared with equilibrium thermodynamics, the theory and practice of far-from-equilibrium dissipative structures remain very crude and rudimentary.

Physics, biology, chemistry, and even the social sciences have stopped at the doorstep of far-from-equilibrium and self-organization; behind the phenomena of order lies a treasure trove of truth that humanity cannot yet enter. When George M. Whitesides envisioned the future of the discipline and industry of chemistry in "Reinventing Chemistry"[^1], he pointed out that after the 1990s the historical melody of chemistry shifted from one "discovery" after another to "iteration" and "improvement"; and in the future, "atoms, molecules, and reactions" will not be enough to capture chemistry. The chemistry of the future should engage with the origin of life, explain the molecular basis of life, study dissipative systems such as oceans, atmospheres, and flames, try to answer how the brain works, understand the influence of the microbiome on health, and guide drug design rationally, among other things.
