Transparency is an ideal property long neglected in software engineering, and a weak spot in today's internet-industry technical management practices. The transparency discussed here mainly refers to the visibility, explainability, and degree of control that a component's internal implementation offers to its engineering team — in other words, its [white-box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_box_(software_engineering)) index.

The fewer third-party black boxes a project depends on top-down, the more transparent its many components across all abstraction levels become to the engineering team. High transparency means high maintainability, being highly hackable, and being disassemblable at any time: whenever you locate a performance bottleneck at any abstraction level, you can zoom in without any shackles, then modify and refactor. Habitually reaching for third-party dependencies — even ones that are widely adopted, or even prestigious projects approaching industry-standard status — still injects the risk of an imperfect match between the implementation and your actual requirements.

An important heuristic design principle of high-performance computing is specialization, which obviously includes software specialization — creating new solutions that most directly fit new, real requirements (direct solutions to the needs), i.e., reinventing the wheel.

Engineers in the Chinese internet industry are overly cautious about reinventing the wheel, treating it as an anti-pattern. The moment a problem arises, they immediately jump to technology selection, having formed an unthinking path dependence on third-party dependencies. Lacking the acuity and hunger to perceive the novelty and uniqueness of a problem, they naturally confine themselves to technology selection and can never achieve real technical innovation — innovation in computing systems is rooted in real requirements; it imposes structure on unstructured real-world needs and creates new forms of computation and storage. Take full-text search as an example: [Meituan Waimai's search uses Elasticsearch](https://tech.meituan.com/2022/11/17/elasicsearch-optimization-practice-based-on-run-length-encoding.html), while [WeChat's chat search uses SQLite](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/608082104). In both cases, no in-house high-performance retrieval engine was chosen, leading to a mismatch between the business scenario and the classic solution; the so-called optimizations after hitting performance bottlenecks later on amounted to nothing more than hacking the third-party library to make it fit the original requirements a bit better. For instance, Lucene is simply not an in-memory index, to the point that even a scenario as small-scale as searching a takeout shop's catalog of items cannot guarantee real-time performance. As for SQLite, it of course does not support table sharding plus parallel search by default — yet doing simple sharding and scatter-gather parallel search over a large database to boost performance is, in fact, the natural and obvious solution.

Treating wheel-reinvention as such a forbidding path is truly lamentable. After hitting the performance wall, one burrows into someone else's dusty pile of old code to patch and tinker — the wasted effort and the extra pain generated far exceed what it would have taken to implement a small and beautiful in-memory search index in C++/Rust in the first place (for example, [pisa](https://github.com/pisa-engine/pisa)). An in-house retrieval engine can beat Lucene, SQLite, and Postgres with very little code, easily support multi-threaded real-time index updates without sacrificing index performance, and be deeply cache-optimized for modern physical machines — reducing inter-core communication and remote memory access, and pushing the performance of modern hardware to its extreme on a memory-bound application like search.
