Noam Chomsky said: human ignorance can be divided into mysteries and problems.

Well said. To fight ignorance, human rationality can likewise be divided into induction and deduction.

The former infers mysteries; the latter solves problems.

The former is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_theory) but uncomputable; the latter is [computable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory) but incomplete.

## What Is a Problem?
What is a problem? In this article it refers to solvable problems — those with well-defined inputs and outputs, for which a deterministic algorithm exists that computes step by step and eventually arrives at the correct result, such as the [eight queens puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_queens_puzzle) and the [Hamiltonian path problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_path_problem).

## What Is a Mystery?
What is a mystery? In this article it refers to unsolvable problems — those that either require infinite computational resources or possess undecidable properties (undecidability is essentially uncomputability), such as the [undecidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undecidable_problem) [halting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halting_problem) posed by Turing, or the "true but unprovable" [Gödel sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems) constructed by Gödel. Another example: the confidence computation and prediction of pure Bayesianism must simultaneously consider infinitely many predictive theories; real computers can handle neither the infinitude of the computation nor the non-termination of its algorithmic branches — not all computations terminate, and some even grow more complex as they run, never coming to rest.

## What Is Deduction?
What is deduction? In this article it refers to [deductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning). Formal systems (or, one might say, deductive devices) such as Euclidean geometry, elementary arithmetic, the Turing machine, and the [λ-calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda_calculus) are systems composed of a set of axioms and a series of inference rules, allowing new theorems to be derived from the axioms.

Mathematical logic and deductive reasoning possess a natural beauty. When we studied Euclidean geometry as children, we must all have marveled at this formalist beauty: from five concise axioms one can derive a system so complex, vast, and profound, yet indubitably correct. Deduction seemed to be the key to truth.

Naturally, some mathematicians in history hoped to found all mathematical theories on a finite and complete set of axioms, and to provide a proof that these axioms are consistent. In the 1920s, Hilbert, leader of the formalist school, hoped to build a formal proof system expressing all mathematical statements in a symbolic-logic language that strictly follows rules of operation, giving mathematics — then facing multiple [paradox crises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics#Foundational_crisis) — a secure foundation. This was [Hilbert's program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%27s_program), a great attempt by human rationality to fight mystery. Hilbert said: "We must know, and we shall know!" That was the formalists' battle cry. They firmly believed that mathematics has completeness (in formalized mathematics, all mathematical statements are provable), consistency (in formalized mathematics, no contradiction exists), decidability (there is always an algorithm that can decide the truth of a mathematical statement), and conservativeness (proofs do not depend on ideal objects such as uncountable sets). As long as these four assumptions hold, no matter how grand and boundless reality is, no matter how unattainable truth may seem, humanity, holding the key of deductive reasoning, could prove all truths.

Yet Hilbert's program was terminated by Gödel, Turing, and Church before it even began. In the 1930s, Gödel proposed "general recursive functions," Turing constructed the "Turing machine," and Church proposed the "λ-calculus"; the three independently formalized computability: λ-computability is equivalent to Turing-machine computability, which in turn is equivalent to general recursiveness. [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems#First_incompleteness_theorem) proved that (1) a formal system containing elementary arithmetic, if consistent, is incomplete; and (2) the consistency of such a system cannot be proved within the system itself. Turing proposed the halting problem, proving that we cannot decide whether a Turing machine will halt. The undecidability of the halting problem implies the undecidability of mathematics. Were the halting problem decidable, Turing machines could be used to prove any mathematical problem, because in theory we could write a Turing machine program that constructs all theorems starting from the axioms, checking each generated theorem against the target problem — say, the twin prime conjecture — and if it matches, the program halts; if not, it never halts.

## What Is Induction?
What is induction? In this article it refers to [statistical inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_inference), broadly the process of using data analysis to infer properties of a probability distribution.

Statistics has the dispute between the Bayesian and frequentist schools. Bayesians hold that the model parameters to be estimated are random variables carrying the model's subjectivity, while the data used to estimate those parameters are, conversely, fixed constants; discussing the probability distribution of the observed data is meaningless — probability is interpreted as the degree of belief, given current knowledge, that an event will occur. Frequentists hold that events themselves possess some objective randomness; probability is a definite value, and discussing the distribution of a probability is meaningless — probability is merely the limit of frequency as the number of repeated experiments approaches infinity. Bayesian theory first originated in 1763; afterward it was regarded condescendingly by the frequentist school as a supplement to orthodox methods that were already good enough, or simply as an interesting but mistaken attempt. It was not taken seriously again by academia until the end of the last century. Part of the reason is that computers replaced calculators as the new computing tool, overcoming the computational difficulties of Bayesian methods. Before general-purpose electronic computers appeared, the frequentist practice of making special assumptions for particular problems was clearly more economical and often quite effective — except that these artificial assumptions were hard to falsify and hard to prove.

Ultimately, frequentist inductive inference can at best be considered an approximation of Bayesian inference, and part of that approximation is a wrong approximation. CERN's discovery of the Higgs boson via the Large Hadron Collider was actually based on this: assuming the Higgs boson does not exist, the p-value of the observed data is < 0.00003%; the threshold looked low enough, so CERN's researchers — and all of human society — accepted the existence of the Higgs boson. Where did this 0.0003% threshold come from? Like the p-value < 5% thresholds in humanities and social-science research, it comes from intuition and artificial assumptions. So-called science is built on foundations this flimsy. Even in an occasion as extremely rigorous as physics experiments, p-value-based statistical analysis can still produce illusions. The pentaquark state of 2003 was announced as discovered because of its p-value, yet was later rejected when the original experiment could not be reproduced. [Regina Nuzzo criticized the abuse of p-values in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/506150a), using examples to demonstrate the widespread problem in scientific research of statistically significant p-value results being irreproducible.

In the 1960s, the pure Bayesian Solomonoff went beyond Gödel's incompleteness theorems and beyond the Church–Turing thesis, proposing a computational formalization of Bayesian theory — [Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonoff%27s_theory_of_inductive_inference) — and proving that the theory is complete and uncomputable. Solomonoff made a striking observation: completeness and computability cannot both be had. Any theory, so long as it is computable, cannot detect all regularities. And a theory with completeness, like Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference, is necessarily uncomputable.

The completeness of Solomonoff's theory means that Bayes' formula (shown here in simplified form) P(θ|d) = P(d|θ)P(θ)/P(d) can almost be regarded as the formula of truth. Deductive reasoning is then the correct special case of Bayesian reasoning in scenarios where truth values are certain: when P(d) = 1, P(θ|d) = P(d|θ)P(θ)/P(d) in probabilistic language naturally degenerates into the classical-logic statement d => θ, i.e., d implies θ.

When we use if-else conditionals in a computer programming language and, no matter how hard we try, still cannot write a program whose results are satisfactory, we are usually facing a mystery, not a problem.

In such scenarios, the means of classical logic are exhausted; deduction is clearly not the key to truth — but we can envision Solomonoff's demon.

The pure Bayesianism represented by Solomonoff's demon is admittedly uncomputable, yet it can be approximately simulated by various means; the typical structure is the deep neural network, which uses billions, tens of billions, hundreds of billions of parameters/weights to simulate Bayesian inference in a bloated, clumsy way.

On decision-making and game-playing tasks, reinforcement learning has achieved astonishing results compared to rule-based methods. Current reinforcement learning can basically solve any human game: DeepMind's StarCraft AI AlphaStar defeated MaNa, Dota 2's OpenAI Five defeated OG, and Go's AlphaGo defeated Ke Jie. This was almost impossible for earlier rule-based methods.

On understanding and generation tasks, large language models, compared to traditional NLP methods, achieved extra effects beyond their training objectives, with all sorts of surprises emerging — such as single-step logical reasoning ability, and logical reasoning ability for certain simple composite problems.

The theoretical foundation of computation based on Turing machines and the λ-calculus has stood unmoved for decades; once it changes, it implies a deeper transformation — we are witnessing the leap from the deductive reasoning of Turing machines to the statistical inference of neural networks, the qualitative change from "computable but incomplete" to "uncomputable but complete."

The earlier post [Knowledge is Embeddings of Reality](https://jipeng4974.github.io/writeups/reality-knowledge) mentioned that the understanding of audio can be at different depths and from different angles: music information retrieval can use signal-processing rule-based methods, deep neural networks like ResNet, or large models like Whisper. The deeper the understanding, the less precise it is, and the more flexible the applications, the closer to human thinking. That is the perspective of understanding. From the perspective of computation, signal-processing-based systems perform deductive reasoning, and thus have the strongest computability but are naturally incomplete: no matter how many axioms we add to the code, how many execution paths we add, how ingenious the handcrafted features we design, how marvelous the expert insights we inject, there will always be scenarios they fail to cover — indeed, they can only cover a very, very small part, because audio data comes from reality, and raw data possesses extremely high overall complexity and fine structural delicacy, exceeding the limits of human thought and programming; that audio features designed by human experts can account for noise resistance and possess simple robustness is already SOTA. Methods based on deep models, and even large models, are approximations of Bayesian inference: giving up precision and increasing computational cost, they shift from deduction to induction, from convergence to openness; even as mere approximations of Bayes, they obtain a certain degree of completeness in exchange — no longer limited to simple resistance to noise and pitch shifting, they begin to consider audio similarity at the level of melody, emotion, style, and even the linguistic logic of lyrics.

## Cognitive Computing: A Revolution of Humanization and Completion
Reasoning-based algorithms are, in essence, manually writing axioms (prior data, such as magic numbers, mask tables, configuration files) or rules (traditional conditional control and arithmetic computation) for a formal system (λ-calculus, Turing machine), performing correct, efficient, but incomplete deductive reasoning.

Inference-based algorithms are, in essence, using a formal system (computing a neural network is still a formal system — its axioms have become the much larger model weights, and its rules have become combinations of mathematical operators such as matrix multiply-add, normalization, and activation functions) to perform a crude, erroneous, but useful approximation of [Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonoff%27s_theory_of_inductive_inference) (formalized pure Bayesianism).

Statistical inference and model inference are both "inference" in English, yet Chinese uses different words for them — 推断 for statistical inference, 推理 for model inference — and I do not know whether this is a coincidence or the collective unconscious secretly echoing the truth: 推断 is induction, while 推理 is deduction. The Solomonoff inductive inference of pure Bayesianism is nearly omniscient — uncomputable, inexhaustible, never halting — while the model-inference programs we write, with model weights as axioms and combinations of matrix multiply-add, batch normalization, and activation functions as rules, are in essence still based on deductive reasoning, and so is the process of training — or optimization, learning. Therefore, in the AI-application context of computer science, calling inference 推理 (reasoning) is entirely reasonable, just as in the statistical context, calling inference 推断 (statistical inference) is perfectly reasonable.

So-called cognitive computing is the descent of the omniscient soul of pure Bayesianism into the mortal shell of deductive reasoning, remedying the shortcomings of symbolic logic, rule systems, and signal-processing methods in "humanization (align to humanity)" and "completion (pin down reality)."

One can assert that although AI academia and industry are restless and pompous, and are criticized by researchers from traditional disciplines and even from the traditional fields of computing systems, deep learning itself is by no means a niche application, still less a VC-chased toy and gimmick like the Metaverse or Web3. All computing domains — internet services, data-center applications, smart terminals, game AI — will benefit from progress in cognitive computing, and human civilization may well enter a new stage.

In the near future, human civilization may find it hard to make an inch of progress in fundamental physics, materials, and energy, but building a magnificent cognitive-computing edifice upon cognitive science is almost certain, because the hardware progress required by cognitive computing does not constitute a bottleneck — at least not one constrained by spatiotemporal scales and material limits the way colliders, deep-space travel, and controlled nuclear fusion are. After all, consider that even the brain of a tiny bee, less than 2 cubic centimeters in size, possesses complex 3D-environment path-finding and obstacle-avoidance abilities surpassing humanity's current advanced rule-based and navigation-assisted algorithms, as well as innate Bayesian inference ability. The natural structure produced by hundreds of millions of years of biological evolution already exists; we need not worry that such a structure fundamentally does not exist: it is right there, and all we need to do is approximate and imitate it.
